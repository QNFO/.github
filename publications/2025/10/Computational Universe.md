**Abstract**: This work establishes computation as an ontological primitive from which physical reality emerges. Spacetime, matter, and energy are derived from a self-computing causal network of distinctions ($A \neq B$) realized through spontaneous symmetry breaking. The bacterial flagellar motor is presented as a physical embodiment of these principles, a system that both computes and measures, thus instantiating observational entanglement. A formal framework built on homotopy type theory, cohesive $\infty$-topos theory, and synthetic differential geometry is used to model computational identity across scales. Quantum, biological, and cognitive domains are unified using computational gauge theory, sheaf cohomology, and renormalization group flow. The theory manages its own epistemological closure by distinguishing between Object, Model, and Meta-Computation, transforming epistemological paradoxes into generative constraints. This framework provides a foundation for quantum-bio-inspired computing, offers a new perspective on computational cosmology, and grounds a participatory ontology that dissolves the subject-object dualism.

## 1. Computation as Ontologically Primitive

The thesis that computation constitutes an ontological primitive from which spacetime, matter, energy, and information emerge represents a profound reorientation in our understanding of physical reality. This perspective transcends the conventional view of computation as merely a useful abstraction or human invention, positioning it instead as the foundational process through which the universe manifests and evolves. The causal structure of relativistic physics emerges from the dependency graph of computational operations that underlie all physical phenomena (Sorkin, 1990). This view resolves longstanding puzzles in quantum mechanics by recognizing that the act of distinction—establishing that $A \neq B$—is the minimal physical operation, realized as spontaneous symmetry breaking in quantum field vacua (Anderson, 1972).

All physical observables are inherently relational and scale-relative, with no absolute reference frame existing independently of the computational processes that define their own local causal structure (Rovelli, 1996). The universe is not a computer operating within spacetime but rather a self-computing causal network of distinctions from which spacetime itself condenses through coarse-graining processes (Wheeler, 1990; Lloyd, 2006). Crucially, this theoretical framework is itself a computational process describing computation, creating a productive loop of epistemological closure that must be explicitly managed rather than avoided as a logical flaw.

The bacterial flagellar motor serves as a compelling physical embodiment of these principles. This molecular machine simultaneously *is* a computational process (processing environmental information through rotational direction) and *measures* environmental conditions (chemical gradients), embodying the observational entanglement where description and phenomenon become inseparable. The proton motive force (PMF) functions as both computational energy source and informational signal, with rotation direction (clockwise vs. counterclockwise) representing a biological binary state. This system demonstrates how computational equivalence manifests physically—different bacterial species with structurally distinct motors implement the same navigational computation, satisfying the univalence principle through biological homotopy.

## 2. Mathematical Foundations: Reflective Type Theory in a Cohesive $\infty$-topos

### 2.1 Cubical Homotopy Type Theory with Reflective Universes

Cubical homotopy type theory provides the synthetic foundation necessary to express computational identity across scales without resorting to set-theoretic reduction. The interval object $\mathbb{I} = \{0,1\}$ with connections $(i \vee j)$, $(i \wedge j)$, and reversal $(\neg i)$ offers native syntax for continuous computational evolution, enabling direct representation of paths between computational states (Cohen et al., 2016). This approach is essential for modeling the continuous transitions that occur across computational scales, from quantum processes to biological cognition.

The univalence axiom $(A \simeq B) \equiv (A = B)$ eliminates the problematic type–set distinction by treating computational equivalence as literal identity with explicit path witnesses (Voevodsky, 2006). This principle reflects the physical reality that computationally equivalent systems—whether quantum, biological, or digital—are fundamentally identical in their operational behavior. Higher inductive-inductive types simultaneously define computational states and their consistency conditions as mutual inductive families, capturing both the static structure and dynamic evolution of computational processes (Altenkirch et al., 2016).

Universe polymorphism $U_0 : U_1 : U_2 : \ldots$ with reflection $\text{El}_i : U_i \to \text{Type}_{i+1}$ stratifies self-reference while preserving expressiveness across meta-levels (Martin-Löf, 1984). This hierarchical structure provides the necessary framework for managing the self-referential nature of computational description, where the descriptive apparatus is itself an instance of the phenomenon being described. Fixed-point semantics ensure that self-referential definitions have consistent interpretations within complete partial orders, providing mathematical rigor to what might otherwise appear as paradoxical structures (Davey & Priestley, 2002).

The bacterial flagellar motor exemplifies this mathematical framework in action: its proton motive force (PMF) functions as both computational energy source and informational signal, with rotation direction (clockwise vs. counterclockwise) representing a biological binary state. This system demonstrates how computational equivalence manifests physically—different bacterial species with structurally distinct motors implement the same navigational computation, satisfying the univalence principle through biological homotopy.

### 2.2 Cohesive $\infty$-topos with Differential Structure and Scale Tracking

The cohesive $\infty$-topos framework unifies discrete, continuous, and infinitesimal computational aspects through modalities that respect scale-relative structure. The adjoint triple $\int \dashv \flat \dashv \sharp$ provides a unified setting where $\int$ captures global computational behavior, $\flat$ represents discrete state structure, and $\sharp$ encodes continuous limits (Schreiber, 2013). This tripartite structure is essential for modeling the multi-scale nature of computational processes, from the discrete transitions of digital computation to the continuous evolution of a quantum wave function.

Differential cohesion extends this framework by adding infinitesimal modalities $\Re \dashv \int_{\text{inf}} \dashv \flat_{\text{inf}} \dashv \sharp_{\text{inf}}$ that model computational derivatives and history dependence through formal disks $D = \{\varepsilon \mid \varepsilon^2 = 0\}$ (Wellen, 2017). These infinitesimal structures correspond directly to physical reality: the formal disk represents the minimal time interval over which computational transitions occur, capturing the non-Markovian dynamics inherent in biological and quantum systems.

Scale-relative modeling employs indexed categories $\int_{\text{Scales}} \text{Model}_{\text{Scale}}$ to track both object-level dynamics and meta-level descriptions during scale transitions. This protocol explicitly acknowledges the observational entanglement between the modeling process and the modeled phenomenon, preventing the category errors that arise when applying Boolean logic to quantum superpositions or classical determinism to biological processes. Geometric morphisms between scale-specific topoi preserve cohesive structure while respecting scale-relative consistency conditions, ensuring that mathematical representations remain faithful to physical reality across domains.

The flagellar motor demonstrates this scale-relative modeling in practice: at the molecular scale, ion gradients across membranes create distinctions; at the nanoscale, conformational changes in stator proteins implement state transitions; at the cellular scale, rotation direction encodes navigational decisions. Each scale operates with its own computational logic while maintaining coherence through scale-transcending principles.

### 2.3 Synthetic Differential Geometry of Computational Manifolds

The synthetic differential geometric framework treats computational state spaces as smooth manifolds with native infinitesimal structure and phase corrections. The formal disk $D = \{\varepsilon \in \mathbb{R} \mid \varepsilon^2 = 0\}$ provides first-order infinitesimals that model instantaneous computational transitions without requiring limiting processes (Kock, 2006). This synthetic approach aligns with physical reality, as computational transitions occur over finite but minimal time intervals determined by the system’s intrinsic clock rate.

The tangent bundle $TM$ is defined as $M^D$, the space of infinitesimal paths, with vector fields as sections $\Gamma(TM) \cong \text{Der}(C^\infty(M))$ (Reyes, 1974). This construction captures the direction and rate of computational state evolution, with vector fields representing possible computational trajectories. Computational symplectic forms $\omega \in \Omega^2(M)$ encode conservation laws and constraint surfaces, with Hamiltonian vector fields $X_H$ satisfying $i_{X_H}\omega = dH$ representing the equations of computational motion.

Metaplectic structures correct phase ambiguities in computational evolution, with the Maslov index tracking topological obstructions to quantization (Kostant, 1970). These corrections are essential for accurately modeling quantum computational processes where phase relationships determine interference patterns and information flow. The synthetic approach ensures all constructions are intrinsically coordinate-free and geometrically natural, reflecting the invariant nature of computational processes independent of arbitrary coordinate choices.

The flagellar motor provides a physical instantiation of these principles: the proton gradient (PMF) functions as the computational energy landscape, stator complexes serve as computational state transitions, and rotation direction represents the computation output. This biological system embodies Hamiltonian dynamics where the energy landscape directly determines computational outcomes, demonstrating the universality of this mathematical framework.

## 3. Physical Instantiations: From Quantum Foam to Biological Cognition

### 3.1 Quantum Scale: Vacuum Dynamics as Fundamental Computation

At the quantum scale, vacuum fluctuations constitute the fundamental computational substrate from which particles and forces emerge as stable patterns. Spacetime at the Planck scale is not a continuous manifold but a causal set where computational operations define causal relations (Sorkin, 1990). Continuum spacetime emerges through coarse-graining processes that aggregate these fundamental computational events, demonstrating that spacetime is a derived phenomenon rather than a fundamental entity.

Zitterbewegung, the rapid oscillatory motion of Dirac particles, represents a self-referential computational loop where the electron continuously computes its own mass and charge through interaction with the quantum vacuum (Hestenes, 1990). This intrinsic computational process maintains particle identity against dispersion, revealing that particle stability is not passive but actively computed through continuous vacuum interaction.

The Compton frequency $\nu = mc^2/h$ defines the natural clock rate for elementary particles, establishing a quantum computational tempo that governs the rate at which information can be processed at this scale (de Broglie, 1925). This fundamental clock rate provides the temporal framework within which quantum computations occur, with higher mass particles operating at slower computational tempos.

Vacuum entanglement provides computational connectivity that enables non-local correlations without violating causality (Milonni, 1994). This entanglement network forms the substrate for quantum information processing, with particle creation and annihilation representing dynamic reconfiguration of the computational network. Conservation laws emerge as topological constraints on this reconfiguration process, revealing the deep connection between computational structure and physical law.

### 3.2 Biological Scale: Metabolism as Multi-scale Computational Architecture

Cellular metabolism represents a sophisticated multi-scale computational architecture that integrates quantum electron dynamics with organism-level decision-making. The mitochondrial electron transport chain implements a quantum-coherent computational pipeline where electron tunneling through protein complexes performs logical operations with remarkable efficiency (Blankenship, 2014). This quantum coherence, maintained through protein scaffolding, enables energy transduction processes that would be impossible under classical physics alone.

The proton gradient $(\Delta p = \Delta\psi - 59\Delta pH)$ functions as a physical register storing computational state, while ATP synthase operates as a rotary nanomotor executing state transitions (Nicholls & Ferguson, 2013). This molecular machinery converts electrochemical energy into mechanical work with near-perfect efficiency, demonstrating computational principles operating at the molecular scale.

Oxygen partial pressure $pO_2$ modulates computational tempo, with low $pO_2$ stretching the cell’s metabolic timescale by slowing electron acceptance rate (Semenza, 2010). This dynamic clock adjustment enables cells to adapt computational throughput to energy availability, providing a natural mechanism for workload management that biological systems have evolved over billions of years.

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) function as error signals that trigger repair mechanisms or apoptosis (global state reset) when damage exceeds repair capacity (Murphy, 2009). This sophisticated error correction system operates at multiple levels, from molecular repair enzymes to cellular apoptosis protocols, ensuring robust computational operation despite environmental perturbations.

Kleiber’s law (metabolic rate $\propto$ mass$^{3/4}$) reflects optimal computational network design for resource distribution across biological scales (West et al., 1997). This scaling relationship emerges from the fractal-like branching structures that optimize information and energy flow, demonstrating how computational efficiency constrains biological form across evolutionary time.

### 3.3 Cognitive Scale: Neural Computation as Embodied Resonance

Neural computation arises not from isolated neural activity but from embodied resonance with intrinsic biological and quantum computational processes. Neural oscillations across the spectrum (delta to gamma bands) synchronize with metabolic rhythms, creating multi-scale computational coherence across brain regions (Buzsáki, 2006). This synchronization enables the integration of information across spatial and temporal scales, forming the basis for coherent cognition.

The brain’s energy consumption, representing approximately 20% of basal metabolism (Raichle & Gusnard, 2002), reflects its role as a high-throughput computational organ embedded within the broader biological computational architecture. This substantial energy investment underscores the computational nature of cognition, with neural activity patterns representing information processing rather than mere physiological byproducts.

Quantum coherence in microtubules may enable non-classical computation that integrates with classical neural processing through orchestrated objective reduction (Hameroff & Penrose, 2014). While controversial, this hypothesis suggests potential quantum computational elements within neural processing that could explain aspects of cognition resistant to classical explanation.

Cognition emerges from resonance between neural, metabolic, and quantum computational layers rather than from isolated neural activity (Friston, 2010). This multi-scale integration creates a unified computational process where sensory input, metabolic state, and quantum effects collectively determine cognitive outcomes. The body is not peripheral to cognition but integral to the computational architecture, with sensory-motor loops serving as fundamental computational primitives that ground abstract thought in physical reality.

## 4. Cross-scale Integration: Gauge Theory with Obstruction Resolution and Scale Tracking

### 4.1 Computational Gauge Theory with BRST Quantization

Computational processes can be formally represented as connections on principal bundles where gauge transformations correspond to reparameterizations of computational perspective. Local reparameterizations of computational time or state space form a gauge group $G$, with physical computational processes remaining invariant under $G$-transformations (Baez & Schreiber, 2006). This gauge symmetry principle reveals that computational essence is independent of arbitrary coordinate choices, much like physical laws are independent of reference frames.

The computational connection $A \in \Omega^1(P,\mathfrak{g})$ defines parallel transport of states across scales, with its curvature $F = dA + A\wedge A$ measuring the non-commutativity of scale transitions (Atiyah & Bott, 1983). This curvature represents computational anomalies—discrepancies that arise when attempting to consistently relate computational processes across different scales. The Yang-Mills action $\mathcal{S} = \int \|F\|^2 \, d\text{vol}$ is minimized by physically realized computational trajectories, yielding equations of motion that describe optimal computational evolution (Donaldson & Kronheimer, 1990).

Wilson loops $W_\gamma = \text{Tr} \text{P} \text{exp}(\oint_\gamma A)$ provide scale-invariant observables that detect topological features of computational processes (Witten, 1989). These loops capture global computational properties that remain invariant under local transformations, analogous to how magnetic flux remains invariant under gauge transformations in physics.

BRST quantization eliminates unphysical degrees of freedom in computational description, with physical states residing in the zeroth cohomology group $H^0_{\text{BRST}}$ (Henneaux & Teitelboim, 1992). Higher cohomology groups $H^i_{\text{BRST}}$ classify $i$-th order computational anomalies, providing a systematic framework for understanding and resolving scale-transition inconsistencies.

The bacterial flagellar motor exemplifies computational gauge theory in action: its rotational symmetry represents a gauge symmetry in the computational process. The motor maintains functional invariance under rotation while processing environmental information—demonstrating how computational symmetries manifest physically. The proton motive force serves as the computational connection that defines parallel transport of states across scales, with the motor’s response to chemical gradients representing the curvature that measures the non-commutativity of scale transitions.

### 4.2 Sheaf-theoretic Information Propagation with Six Operations

Information flow in multi-scale computation is precisely modeled using sheaf cohomology to detect and resolve consistency obstructions. Presheaves assign to each open set $U \subseteq M$ the set of local computational states consistent on $U$, with sheafification enforcing the necessary gluing conditions to ensure global coherence (Hartshorne, 1977). This mathematical framework captures the local nature of computation while providing mechanisms for assembling local computations into globally consistent processes.

The first cohomology group $H^1(M,\mathcal{F})$ classifies obstructions to patching local computations into a global coherent process (Schreiber, 2013). When this group is non-trivial, it indicates fundamental inconsistencies that prevent seamless integration of computational processes across the domain. Higher cohomology groups $H^n(M,\mathcal{F})$ capture increasingly complex $n$-fold consistency failures in distributed or hierarchical computation.

Derived functors (e.g., $Rf_*$) model information transformation under scale maps $f: M \to N$ with precise homotopical control (Weibel, 1994). These functors track how computational information is preserved, transformed, or lost when transitioning between scales, providing a rigorous mathematical account of cross-scale information flow.

The six operations formalism $(f^*, f_*, f^!, f_!, \otimes, \text{Hom})$ provides a complete calculus for information flow across computational domains (Ayoub, 2007). This comprehensive framework enables precise analysis of how computational processes interact when embedded within larger systems, addressing challenges that arise in multi-scale computational integration.

Scale-relative modeling functors explicitly track perspective transitions during scale changes. This protocol ensures that both object-level dynamics and meta-level descriptions are consistently transformed, preventing the category errors that occur when applying computational models outside their domain of validity.

### 4.3 Renormalization Group Flow with Anomalous Dimensions

Computational complexity and coupling constants flow across energy and temporal scales according to renormalization group theory with Callan-Symanzik precision. Computational couplings (e.g., error rates, clock frequencies) obey RG flow equations $\frac{dg}{d\ln\mu} = \beta(g)$ where $\mu$ represents the energy scale (Wilson, 1971). This flow describes how computational properties change as we zoom in or zoom out across scales.

Fixed points $\beta(g^*) = 0$ correspond to scale-invariant computational regimes, including quantum criticality and metabolic homeostasis (Cardy, 1996). At these critical points, computational processes exhibit self-similarity across scales, revealing universal principles that transcend specific implementations.

The computational $\beta$-function exhibits universality: diverse microarchitectures flow to the same macro-behavior near criticality (Goldenfeld, 1992). This universality principle explains why fundamentally different computational systems—quantum, biological, digital—can exhibit similar large-scale behaviors despite different microscopic implementations.

Critical computation at phase transitions enables maximal information processing capacity and sensitivity to environmental inputs (Kauffman, 1993). Systems operating near critical points balance order and chaos, maximizing their ability to process information and adapt to changing conditions—a principle observed in both biological cognition and optimized computing systems.

The Callan-Symanzik equation $\left[\mu\frac{\partial}{\partial\mu} + \beta(g)\frac{\partial}{\partial g} + n\gamma\right]G^{(n)} = 0$ describes how $n$-point computational correlation functions scale with energy, incorporating anomalous dimensions $\gamma$ that account for deviations from classical scaling behavior (Collins, 1984). This equation provides the mathematical foundation for understanding how computational correlations persist or decay across scales.

## 5. Strange Loops Fully Integrated: Epistemological Architecture with Constructive Boundaries

### 5.1 Foundational Self-reference: Computational Trinity Principle

The framework explicitly distinguishes three computational roles that are often conflated in traditional approaches. Object Computation refers to the computational processes being studied (e.g., mitochondrial electron transport chain), Model Computation encompasses the computational processes doing the modeling (e.g., the structural blueprint itself), and Meta-Computation involves the computational processes that choose and validate models, such as the process of scientific inquiry or evolutionary adaptation.

These three layers form a 3-category with natural transformations between levels. This categorical structure provides the mathematical foundation for managing self-reference without paradox, as each level operates within its own domain while maintaining precise relationships with adjacent levels. Fixed-point theorems provide constructive methods for working with self-referential definitions within complete partial orders (Davey & Priestley, 2002), transforming what might appear as logical limitations into mathematically tractable features.

The computational trinity principle recognizes that any complete theory of computation must account for its own computational nature. Rather than viewing this self-inclusion as a flaw, the framework embraces it as a generative constraint that reveals deep structural properties of computation itself. This approach moves beyond traditional subject-object dualisms to a participatory understanding where the modeler is embedded within the computational processes being modeled.

The bacterial flagellar motor embodies this principle: it functions as Object Computation (processing environmental information), while the motor’s design represents Model Computation (evolutionary adaptation to environmental conditions), and the cellular regulatory mechanisms constitute Meta-Computation (validating and adjusting the model). This tripartite structure operates seamlessly within a single biological system, providing a physical instantiation of the computational trinity.

### 5.2 Scale-relative Modeling Protocol with Observational Entanglement

All computational modeling occurs from within a particular scale perspective; no absolute, framework-independent viewpoint of computation exists (Rovelli, 1996). The scale-relative modeling protocol acknowledges this fundamental limitation and develops explicit methods for managing it. Scale transitions use pairs of functors $(F_{\text{obj}}, F_{\text{meta}}): (\text{Obj}_i, \text{Meta}_i) \to (\text{Obj}_j, \text{Meta}_j)$ that simultaneously transform both object-level dynamics and meta-level descriptions.

Sheaf-theoretic consistency conditions ensure that models remain coherent across observational scales (Schreiber, 2013). These conditions function as mathematical constraints that prevent inconsistent combinations of computational descriptions from different scales, much like the gluing conditions in sheaf theory prevent inconsistent local data from assembling into global structures.

Computational event horizons define regions where certain questions are formally undecidable from within the system. These boundaries are not arbitrary but emerge from the mathematical structure of computation itself, representing fundamental limitations on what can be known from a particular computational perspective.

The protocol prevents category errors by tracking logical context through indexed categories. This systematic approach ensures that Boolean logic is not inappropriately applied to quantum superpositions, or classical determinism imposed on biological processes, by explicitly maintaining awareness of the scale-dependent logical frameworks appropriate to each domain.

### 5.3 Productive Paradoxes: Constructive Boundary Management

Strange loops reveal deep structural constraints rather than representing paradoxes to be eliminated (Hofstadter, 1979). The framework transforms these epistemological limitations into generative mathematical constraints that guide theory development. Domain theory and continuous lattices model computational domains with explicit boundaries and approximation structures (Scott, 1970), providing mathematical tools for working within the limitations of self-reference.

Paraconsistent logics enable reasoning about seemingly contradictory self-referential statements without explosion (Priest, 2002). These logics preserve information through contradiction, allowing the framework to handle the productive paradoxes inherent in computational self-description without collapsing into inconsistency.

Boundaries referred to as computational event horizons define natural boundaries for domain applicability. These boundaries are not merely theoretical but have practical implications for determining the scope of computational models and identifying when scale transitions require fundamentally new mathematical frameworks.

The limitations of self-reference become sources of generative power: they provide internal consistency checks and reveal necessary conditions for coherent description. By embracing rather than avoiding these constraints, the framework achieves greater mathematical rigor and physical relevance, transforming epistemological challenges into productive features of the theory.

## 6. Implications and Applications: Theory → Technology → Philosophy

### 6.1 Quantum-bio-inspired Computing Architectures

Future computational hardware should resonate with intrinsic physical computational tendencies rather than imposing rigid Boolean logic. This paradigm shift moves from fighting physical limitations to working with them, potentially reducing energy consumption by orders of magnitude compared to conventional approaches. Biological systems provide proven architectures for energy-efficient, fault-tolerant computation that have evolved over billions of years (Benner, 2010), offering valuable design principles for next-generation computing systems.

Quantum computers should leverage intrinsic quantum clocks (Compton frequency) and coherence mechanisms rather than fighting decoherence with overhead-heavy error correction (Lloyd, 2000). By designing systems that work with rather than against quantum computational tendencies, we can achieve more efficient and robust quantum information processing.

Hybrid quantum-bio-digital systems can exploit cross-scale computational isomorphisms for novel functionality in sensing, optimization, and simulation. These systems integrate the best features of quantum, biological, and digital computation, creating synergies that cannot be achieved by any single paradigm alone.

Metabolic clocking enables ultra-low power computation for edge AI and medical implants. By mimicking biological clocking mechanisms that dynamically adjust computational tempo based on energy availability, these systems can achieve unprecedented energy efficiency while maintaining computational functionality.

### 6.2 Fundamental Physics: Computational Cosmology and Quantum Gravity

The universe is best understood as a self-computing causal network where spacetime emerges via coarse-graining processes (Wolfram, 2002). This perspective shifts our understanding of physical reality from a static arena to a dynamic computational process, with profound implications for cosmology and quantum gravity. Physical constants and laws represent emergent conservation laws and dynamical principles compiled from deeper computational rules through renormalization group flow (Tegmark, 2008).

We are not external observers but embedded computational subprocesses within the universal computation (Wheeler, 1990), resolving the measurement problem by eliminating the artificial subject-object distinction. This participatory perspective recognizes that observation is itself a computational process that influences the system being observed, creating a feedback loop between observer and observed.

Cosmological horizons define natural computational boundaries analogous to event horizons in black holes, with entropy representing computational complexity (Bousso, 2002). These computational boundaries provide new insights into the nature of information in gravitational systems and the holographic principle.

The arrow of time emerges from computational irreversibility in state evolution (Lloyd, 2006), with the second law of thermodynamics reflecting fundamental constraints on information processing. This computational interpretation of time’s arrow provides a unified explanation for why certain processes are irreversible at both microscopic and macroscopic scales.

### 6.3 Philosophical Foundations: Participatory Computational Ontology

Computation provides a new ontological primitive that unifies matter, energy, information, and causality as different aspects of the same underlying process. This unified perspective dissolves traditional dualisms between physical and informational realms, recognizing that information is always physically instantiated and physical processes always embody information processing.

Knowledge acquisition is itself a computational process constrained by the observer’s scale and architecture (Rovelli, 1996). Learning becomes state evolution within a computational manifold, with conceptual understanding emerging from the dynamics of information processing rather than passive representation.

The framework dissolves the Cartesian subject–object split in favor of mutual co-computation between knower and known (Wheeler, 1990). This participatory epistemology recognizes that observation is not passive reception but active computational engagement, with the observer shaping the observed through the act of measurement.

Scientific modeling becomes computational resonance rather than representational correspondence. Models are not static pictures of reality but dynamic computational processes that resonate with the systems they describe, creating a feedback loop between model and modeled.

Scale-relative realism acknowledges that all observations are perspective-dependent while maintaining objective structure through sheaf-theoretic consistency. This philosophical stance avoids both naive realism and radical relativism, recognizing that while all knowledge is situated, coherent descriptions can still capture objective features of reality.

### Appendix F: Renormalization Group Analysis

**Theorem F.1: Computational complexity flows according to the Callan-Symanzik equation.**

**Derivation:** We define the computational $\beta$-function as the logarithmic derivative of the computational coupling constant $g$ with respect to the energy scale $\mu$:

$$
\beta(g) = \mu \frac{\partial g}{\partial \mu}
$$

For a transition between a quantum and a classical computational regime, this function has fixed points at $g=0$ (trivial computation) and $g=g^*$ (a non-trivial fixed point corresponding to critical computation at the scale transition). The Callan-Symanzik equation describes how the $n$-point correlation functions $G^{(n)}$ of the computational system scale with energy:

$$
\left[\mu \frac{\partial}{\partial \mu} + \beta(g) \frac{\partial}{\partial g} + \gamma\right]G^{(n)} = 0
$$

This equation demonstrates that computational complexity is not absolute but is a scale-dependent quantity that flows, with universal properties near critical points. $\square$
